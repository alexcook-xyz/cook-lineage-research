## Proof Summary: What Is Proven vs. What Remains Open

- **What We Have Completely Proven:** Between **1750 and 1771**, a single man named **George Cook** and his wife **Sarah** owned and lived on a **400-acre plantation** on the head branches of Harris's Creek (first in Amelia County, then in Prince Edward County when created in 1754). We have accounted for **every single acre** (`150 acres` sold to Alexander Guill in 1768 + `250 acres` sold to Lovel Wilkerson in 1771 = `400 acres`), verified his age-or-infirmity **levy exemption in 1760**, and traced his departures under merchant debt suits in **1769–1771**.
- **Why He Fits Above Generation 8 (Not As Gen 8):** Because George Cook was already an adult landowner in November 1750 (`born before 1729`) and was **excused from paying county poll taxes in September 1760** (`likely born c. 1700–1715`), he would have been 115–135 years old when Generation 8 (`George Cook Sr.`) died in 1835. Moreover, in Sept 1760 his household had only **1 tithable (`George Cook one`)**, meaning any surviving son in that house was under age 16 (`born after Sept 1744`)—fitting the exact age bracket for Generation 8.
- **What Remains Open (`~45–55%` Link):** His 1771 deed calls him *"of Prince Edward"* without naming children or his next county. We still need a documentary link bridging him (or his son) across the **1771–1782** gap into Sandy Creek, [Pittsylvania County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pittsylvania_County,_Virginia "Virginia's largest county by land area, formed in 1767 from Halifax County along the Dan River borderlands with its seat at Chatham."), alongside a final check of **Spotsylvania Order Book 1749–1755** (**Issue #13**).

## Biographical Overview & Migration Trail

**George Cook** was likely born in the first two decades of the eighteenth century (`c. 1700–1715`) in the [Colony of Virginia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colony_of_Virginia "First permanent English colony in North America (founded 1607 at Jamestown), governed by the British Crown until 1776."). The strongest pre-1750 trace places a **George Cook** and his wife **Sarah** in St. George's Parish, [Spotsylvania County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spotsylvania_County,_Virginia "Central Virginia county founded in 1721 and named for colonial governor Alexander Spotswood, with its seat at Spotsylvania Courthouse."), between **1741 and 1748**. There he served as a juror (4 August 1741), acquired 800 acres via deeds from George Stubblefield and John Sartin plus a 400-acre Crown patent (1742–1743), sold off his Spotsylvania land with Sarah (`1743–1744`), and presented a runaway-slave claim on **7 June 1748** immediately above **Elexius Musick**—the same Spotsylvania neighbor who later moved to Sandy Creek in Pittsylvania County and sold land to George Cooke Jr. in 1780.

By **3 November 1750**, George Cook received a **400-acre Crown patent** by 40-shilling cash purchase (`Patents 29:316–317`) on the western frontier of Amelia County at the head branches of **Harris's Creek** (upper [Appomattox River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appomattox_River "157-mile tributary of the James River flowing through central and southside Virginia past Farmville and Petersburg to City Point.") drainage), bordering Charles Hudson (`Patents 18:44–45`) and Thomas Williamson (`Patents 28:212–213`, sold to Thomas Lockett III). In **September 1751**, Amelia Court ordered him and his male tithables to maintain the [Rolling Road](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rolling_road "Colonial-era dirt track used to roll heavy wooden casks (hogsheads) of cured tobacco from inland plantations down to river landings and inspection warehouses.") across nearby Vaughan's Creek beside neighbors **Peter Coffee**, **William Mills**, **Charles Simmons**, and **Elkanah Jennings** (*Amelia OB 2:371–372*).

When Prince Edward County was carved out of Amelia in **1754**, Cook's Harris's Creek plantation fell into **St. Patrick's Parish, Prince Edward County**. On **4 June 1760**, the parish processioners recorded his tract alongside neighbors **Edward Grisham** (`Gresham`) and **Lockett**. Three months later, on **9 September 1760**, Prince Edward Court listed him for **one tithable** beside **Peter Coffee** and formally **excused him from paying the county levy** (*PE OB 2*), an exemption granted for advanced age or physical infirmity.

During the 1760s, Cook faced recurring merchant credit suits from Glasgow trading houses (`Archibald Buchanan & Co.`, `Alexander Speirs & Co.`, and `John Pleasants / Routledge & Co.`). When sued by Pleasants on a 1766 bond in **August 1769** and **June 1770**, his former 1751 road-crew neighbors **Peter Coffee** and **William Mills** (along with Henry Walker) stood as his appearance bail. To settle up and relocate, Cook liquidated his 400-acre plantation in two documented sales:
1. On **18 April 1768** (*PE DB 3:366–368*), he sold the southern **150 acres** to **Alexander Guill** for £13, and on **20 May 1768** (*PE OB 4:109–110*) his wife **Sarah Cook** appeared in court to relinquish her dower right.
2. After remaining on the northern half through late 1769 (confirmed by Guill's 3 Oct 1769 trust deed `"adjoining to George Cooke"`), on **6 April 1771** (*PE DB 5:67–68*, proved 16 September 1771) he sold the remaining **250 acres**—explicitly reciting his **`3 November 1750` (`MDCCL`)** patent and calling it *"all that tract of Land he the sd. George Cook now possesses and lives on"*—to **Lovel Wilkerson** for £30. No wife signed or relinquished dower in 1771, after which George Cook vanishes entirely from Prince Edward records.

## Occupation & Everyday Life in Piedmont Virginia (1740s–1771)

### What Did He Do for a Living?
Surviving courthouse books do **not** attach an artisan trade title (such as carpenter, blacksmith, or shoemaker) to George Cook. Instead, every surviving record identifies him as a **yeoman farmer / small frontier planter** operating inside colonial Virginia's tobacco-and-corn economy:
- **Smallholder Farming by Household Labor:** In **1760**, Prince Edward County taxed George Cook for **`one` tithable** (`George Cook one`). Because every free white male aged 16+ and every enslaved worker aged 16+ was taxed as a tithable, holding **only one tithable** in 1760 means he was **not** running a large slave-worked plantation in Prince Edward at that point—he worked his 400-acre creek tract primarily with his own family's hands (unlike his earlier 1742–1748 Spotsylvania years, when he briefly held 800 acres and had `male tythes` ordered to road work in 1751).
- **Tobacco & Rolling Road Maintenance:** His 1751 assignment to maintain the **Rolling Road** over Vaughan's Creek tied his farm directly to tobacco agriculture. Inland planters packed cured tobacco leaf into ~1,000-pound wooden **hogsheads**, fitted axles through the barrels, and rolled them along ridge-top rolling roads toward river inspection warehouses along the Appomattox and James Rivers.
- **Merchant Credit Economy:** Between 1762 and 1770, George Cook was sued repeatedly by Glasgow Scottish merchant firms (`Archibald Buchanan & Co.`, `Alexander Speirs & Co.`, and `John Pleasants / Routledge & Co.`). In [Southside](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southside_%28Virginia%29 "Historic cultural and agricultural region of Virginia south of the James River, centered on tobacco farming during the colonial era.") Virginia during the 1760s, small planters rarely held hard coin; instead, they bought salt, iron tools, textiles, gunpowder, and household goods on year-round store credit, settling each fall with cured tobacco hogsheads—and frequently landing in county court whenever tobacco prices dropped or harvests fell short.

### Daily Life on the Harris's Creek Frontier (`1750–1771`)
When George Cook patented his 400 acres in November 1750 along the rolling hills of Virginia's [Piedmont](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piedmont_%28United_States%29 "Plateau region of rolling hills between the Atlantic Coastal Plain and the Blue Ridge Mountains, running across central Virginia."), he stood near the active edge of colonial settlement:
- **Landscape & Housing:** Harris's Creek sits in an oak, hickory, and pine ridge-and-valley watershed draining into the upper Appomattox River near modern Prospect, Virginia. Newly patented 400-acre frontier tracts typically consisted of thick hardwood forest broken by small clearings around a one- or two-room log or timber-frame dwelling, a tobacco curing barn, a corn crib, split-rail worm fences to keep free-ranging hogs and cattle out of the fields, and spring-fed garden patches.
- **Work & Diet:** A small Piedmont family split its year between felling and girdling timber to open fresh tobacco ground, transplanting and topping tobacco, tending Indian corn, wheat, and pumpkins, and letting hogs forage on acorns in the woods for autumn salting. Cornmeal mush/bread, salt pork, cider, and kitchen greens formed the daily staple.
- **Civic Duty, Parish Life & Mutual Aid:** Even on a sparse frontier, life revolved around county court and Anglican parish governance. Freeholders served on road crews twice a year (`Amelia OB 2:372`), had their land lines walked every four years by parish processioners (`St. Patrick's Vestry Book, p. 62`), petitioned court for age/infirmity levy exemptions (`PE OB 2`), and relied closely on neighbors for survival—as shown when George Cook's 1751 road-crew neighbors **Peter Coffee** and **William Mills** still stood bail for him in court nearly twenty years later (`1769–1770`). Meanwhile, his **£13** sale of 150 acres (`1768`) and **£30** sale of 250 acres (`1771`) illustrate a modest, cash-strapped small farm rather than wealthy gentry—and help explain why elderly frontier families sold worn-out tobacco land and moved southwest toward cheaper backcountry tracts like Pittsylvania County.

## Remaining Open Questions & Locked Records

1. **Where did George Cook go after September 1771?** Exhaustive sweeps of LVA Chancery (`182` suits), *Publick Claims*, *Heads of Families* (`1782–1785`), and unlocked `PE OB 5–7` show he never returned to Prince Edward. Bridging the `1771–1782` decade requires checking Halifax/Lunenburg/Pittsylvania pre-1782 tithables and **Pittsylvania `WB 1:293–294`** (**Issues #4, #10, #13**).
2. **Did he come from Spotsylvania in 1748–1750?** Full inspection of **Spotsylvania `Order Book 1749–1755`** and the locked **Spotsylvania `General Index to Wills` (`Film 34055`)** will test whether the 1741–1748 couple moved south or remained in Spotsylvania (**Issue #13**).
3. **Separate Sarah Cook Note (*PE WB 2:42–43*):** A `Sarah [+] Cook` of Prince Edward wrote a will dated **1 February 1784** (verified on close zoom, proved 17 Oct 1785, admin. `William Mitchel`) naming daughters **`Conney Micel`** and **`Ester Michell`** (`"I give and [be]queth Ester Michell..."`), with no husband or sons named ([scan](../../evidence/va-colonial-lva/PE_WB2_p42-43_Sarah_Cook_Will_1764_1785.jpg)). Do not merge with George Cook's wife Sarah without proof.
4. **In-Person Locked Reels:** FamilySearch Center access is required for Prince Edward *General Index to Deeds* (`DGS 7897266/7897267`), Prince Edward *Will Book 1* (`DGS 7645993/7644207`), *PE Land Tax 1782–1790* (LVA microfilm only), and all Amelia *Deed & Will Indexes* (`DGS 8140735, 8248758, 8358019, 7856504, 7675972`).
